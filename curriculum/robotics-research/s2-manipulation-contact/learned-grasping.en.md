# Learned grasping

## Setup and notation

`s2-grasp-mechanics` and `s2-tactile-contact-estimation` are assumed. Two of their results in
particular are the starting point.

- The failure mode of `s2-grasp-mechanics` R2 — that the Ferrari–Canny metric $Q$ depends on the
  **force normalization and the characteristic length for the torque**, so it cannot be compared
  across papers. Here that metric becomes the **label**.
- The Jensen gap of `s2-tactile-contact-estimation` R3 — that the expectation of a non-linear
  function must not be confused with the function of the expectation. The same error recurs in
  reporting success rates.

Also assumed: the metrics of binary classification (AUC, Brier score, calibration error ECE), the
Bayes-optimal classifier, and total variation distance. Network architectures and training
techniques are **not** needed — this lesson is about **labels and distributions**, not architecture.

Notation (in addition to module S2's):

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $g$ | a grasp candidate (pose plus opening width) |
| $o$ | the observation (point cloud or depth image) |
| $y \in \{0,1\}$ | the grasp success label |
| $p(g, o)$ | the true success probability $\mathbb{P}[y = 1 \mid g, o]$ |
| $\rho$ | the label flip probability (symmetric noise) |
| $\hat p$ | the Bayes-optimal prediction for the noisy label |
| $Q(g)$ | the nominal Ferrari–Canny metric (`s2-grasp-mechanics` R2) |
| $\Pi(g)$ | the **robust metric** — success probability marginalized over pose perturbation |
| $\sigma$ | standard deviation of the pose perturbation |
| $P_{sim}, P_{real}$ | the simulated and real observation distributions |

**The problem.** An analytic metric requires exact contact points and normals
(`s2-grasp-mechanics` R1), and a real robot does not have them. So the modern approach **generates
labels in simulation with an analytic metric** and regresses success probability from point clouds.
That pipeline has three gaps:

$$
\underbrace{Q(g)}_{\text{metric}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{(i) marginalize perturbation}\ }\;
\underbrace{\Pi(g)}_{\text{robust metric}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{(ii) label noise}\ }\;
\underbrace{y}_{\text{label}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{(iii) covariate shift}\ }\;
\underbrace{\text{real success rate}}_{\text{the reported number}}
  \tag{1}
$$

(i) is R1, (ii) is R2 and (iii) is R3. **All three are independent of architecture and are properties
of the data.**

Two assumptions, stated explicitly.

- **(A1) symmetric label noise** — the flip probability is a constant $\rho < 1/2$ independent of
  class and input. Asymmetric and input-dependent noise is in the failure modes of §R2.
- **(A2) covariate shift only** — $P(y \mid g, o)$ is the same in simulation and reality and only
  $P(o)$ differs. If the contact physics differs this fails, and then no reweighting helps (§R3).

## Key results

The bracket is the derivation depth (D0 cited / D1 sketched / D2 full derivation / D3 proof).

- **R1 (separating the robust metric from the nominal one) [D2]** — the robust metric that
  marginalizes a pose perturbation $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$,
  $$\Pi(g) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\big[\mathbb{1}\{g + \epsilon \text{ is force closure}\}\big],
    \tag{2}$$
  may **agree in rank** with the nominal $Q(g)$ and still **disagree in its verdict**. Measured on
  the two-contact family of the unit disc, the Spearman correlation is $1.000$ (zero rank
  inversions), yet the two configurations the nominal test calls **not** force closure have robust
  success rates of $0.470$ and $0.195$. **Labelling by the sign of $Q$ throws away every grasp that
  works about half the time.**
- **R2 (two laws of label noise) [D2]** — under (A1),
  $$\hat p = (1-2\rho)\,p + \rho
    \qquad\text{(calibration shrinks towards the mean)},
    \tag{3}$$
  $$\mathrm{AUC}_{obs} - \tfrac12 = (1-2\rho)\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{true} - \tfrac12\right)
    \qquad\text{(the measured AUC shrinks; balanced base rate)}.
    \tag{4}$$
  (3) makes the **absolute probability untrustworthy** and (4) makes **AUC comparisons across
  datasets meaningless** — a difference in AUC between two benchmarks with different label noise may
  be a difference in $\rho$, not in the model. The ranking itself (the order a model assigns to
  grasps) is **preserved**, since (3) is a monotonically increasing affine map.
- **R3 (a bound on the sim-to-real gap, and how loose it is) [D2]** — under (A2), for a loss
  $\ell \in [0, 1]$,
  $$\big|\mathbb{E}_{P_{real}}[\ell] - \mathbb{E}_{P_{sim}}[\ell]\big|
    \;\le\; \mathrm{TV}(P_{real}, P_{sim}),
    \tag{5}$$
  and correcting by importance weights inflates the variance by the $\chi^2$ divergence. For
  high-dimensional observations (point clouds) $\mathrm{TV} \approx 1$ is easy to reach, so **(5) is
  almost always vacuous** — which is why practice abandons the bound and widens the support of
  $P_{sim}$ by domain randomization.
- **R4 (how to read a reported success rate) [D1]** — a paper's "success rate of $x\%$" depends on
  (a) the object set, (b) the pose distribution, (c) the definition of success (lift and hold for how
  long?) and (d) the number of retries allowed, and without all four it **cannot be compared** with
  another paper. It is the same structural problem as "an unspecified normalization of $Q$" in
  `s2-grasp-mechanics`.

## Derivation

### R1 — from the nominal to the robust metric

**Why marginalize.** The test of `s2-grasp-mechanics` R1 requires exact contact points $p_i$ and
normals $\hat n_i$. In reality there is a perception error $\pm\delta$, so "is this grasp force
closure" has no answer, and the question that does have one is **"what is the probability that it is
force closure under the perturbation distribution"**. That is the $\Pi$ of (2), and it is exactly
the form of Dex-Net's robust metric.

**How $Q$ and $\Pi$ relate.** $Q$ is (by (3) of `s2-grasp-mechanics`) the distance from the origin
to a facet of the wrench hull, so it is a first-order surrogate for the **margin** against
perturbation. Hence there is a **monotone tendency**: a larger $Q$ goes with a larger $\Pi$, and the
reproduction task measures a Spearman correlation of $1.000$ (zero rank inversions) on the
two-contact family of the unit disc. **The verdicts differ, however**: $\Pi$ is continuous while the
sign of $Q$ is discrete.

| $\theta_2-\theta_1$ | 3.142 | 2.642 | 2.600 | 2.242 | 2.200 | 1.942 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nominal $Q$ | $+0.2981$ | $+0.1356$ | $+0.1223$ | $+0.0086$ | $-0.0045$ | $-0.0864$ |
| robust $\Pi$ ($\sigma = 0.25$) | 0.9925 | 0.8700 | 0.8600 | 0.5550 | 0.4700 | 0.1950 |

($\mu = 0.5$, with $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25^2)$ perturbation on the contact angles, 400 samples.)
**Look at the two columns with $Q < 0$** — the nominal verdict is "not force closure" while the
robust success rates are $0.47$ and $0.195$. Making binary labels from the sign of $Q$ makes these
grasps **all negative**, and the model never learns about "grasps that work about half the time".

**Rank is preserved and the verdict is not — why that matters.** Grasp **selection** (pick the best
of several candidates) needs only the ranking, so nominal $Q$ suffices. But **rejection** (is this
grasp worth attempting?) needs the absolute probability, which $Q$ cannot give. And since the
positive/negative ratio of a dataset is set by the threshold used to generate labels, **a success
rate reported without its threshold cannot be interpreted.**

**Where the assumption breaks**: (2) assumes the perturbation distribution
$\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$ is **known**. Real perception error is anisotropic (larger in depth) and
depends on the object and the lighting. Underestimating $\sigma$ makes $\Pi$ optimistic;
overestimating it makes all grasps look alike and the metric loses its information. And the zero rank
inversions on the two-contact family is **a result about that family only** — with three or more
contacts an inversion is possible where one contact is nearly redundant, and finding one is problem
P8.

### R2 — two laws of label noise

**(3) shrinkage of calibration.** The true label $y$ is flipped independently with probability
$\rho$ to give the observed label $\tilde y$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}[\tilde y = 1 \mid g, o]
  &= \mathbb{P}[y = 1](1-\rho) + \mathbb{P}[y = 0]\rho \\
  &= p(1-\rho) + (1-p)\rho = (1-2\rho)p + \rho,
\end{aligned}
$$

which is (3). For $\rho < 1/2$ we have $1-2\rho > 0$, so the map is **monotonically increasing and
affine**: (a) the ranking is preserved and (b) the values shrink towards $1/2$. The size of the
shrinkage is $|\hat p - p| = \rho|1 - 2p|$, so it is **largest for confident predictions** ($p$ near
0 or 1) — the "almost certainly succeeds" region is damaged most.

The expected calibration error is $\mathbb{E}[\rho|1-2p|] = \rho\,\mathbb{E}|1-2p|$. In the
distribution of the reproduction task $\mathbb{E}|1-2p| \approx 0.507$, so
ECE $\approx 0.507\rho$, and the measurements at $\rho = 0.05/0.1/0.2/0.4$ are
$0.0270/0.0516/0.1018/0.2028$ — matching the predictions $0.0254/0.0507/0.1014/0.2028$.

**(4) shrinkage of the measured AUC.** Holding the score $s$ fixed (H2) and measuring AUC with noisy
labels, the conditional densities become **mixtures of the clean conditional and the marginal**.
Writing the positive rate as $\pi = \mathbb{E}[p]$ and the noisy positive rate as
$\tilde\pi = (1-2\rho)\pi + \rho$, equation (3) gives

$$
f_{\tilde 1} = \underbrace{\frac{(1-2\rho)\pi}{\tilde\pi}}_{=:a} f_1
  + \underbrace{\frac{\rho}{\tilde\pi}}_{=:c} f,
\qquad
f_{\tilde 0} = \underbrace{\frac{(1-2\rho)(1-\pi)}{1-\tilde\pi}}_{=:b} f_0
  + \underbrace{\frac{\rho}{1-\tilde\pi}}_{=:d} f,
$$

with $a + c = b + d = 1$ and $f = \pi f_1 + (1-\pi)f_0$. Writing
$A(u,v) = \mathbb{P}[S_u > S_v]$, we have $A(f,f) = \frac12$,
$A(f_1, f) = \frac{\pi}{2} + (1-\pi)A$ and $A(f, f_0) = \frac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi A$ (with
$A := \mathrm{AUC}_{true} = A(f_1,f_0)$), and collecting the four terms gives

$$
\mathrm{AUC}_{obs} - \tfrac12 \;=\; K\left(\mathrm{AUC}_{true} - \tfrac12\right),
\qquad K = ab + ad(1-\pi) + cb\,\pi .
  \tag{4a}
$$

**With a balanced positive rate ($\pi = \frac12$), $K$ collapses to exactly $1-2\rho$**: then
$\tilde\pi = \frac12$, so $a = b = 1-2\rho$ and $c = d = 2\rho$, and

$$
K = (1-2\rho)^2 + 2\rho(1-2\rho) = (1-2\rho)\big[(1-2\rho) + 2\rho\big] = 1-2\rho,
$$

which is (4). The positive rate of the reproduction task is $0.4992$, so (4) holds to measurement
precision: with $\mathrm{AUC}_{true} = 0.9068$ the prediction
$0.5 + (1-2\rho)(0.4068)$ gives $0.8661/0.8254/0.7441/0.5814$ at
$\rho = 0.05/0.1/0.2/0.4$, matching the measurements
$0.8662/0.8252/0.7432/0.5810$ to within $10^{-3}$. **If the training labels are noisy too**, the
model output has already shrunk once by (3), so the same factor is applied twice and it becomes
$(1-2\rho)^2$.

**The practical conclusion.** The same model gives different AUCs on two benchmarks with different
$\rho$. So **comparing models by AUC requires the same label-generation procedure.** For synthetic
labels (an analytic metric plus perturbation marginalization), $\rho$ is set by the perturbation
distribution and the threshold, so a paper that reports neither has an AUC incomparable with any
other. **The problem of "an unspecified normalization of $Q$" in `s2-grasp-mechanics` repeats one
layer up.**

**Where the assumption breaks**: if the symmetry of (A1) fails (say the simulator overestimates
success so $\rho_{0\to1} > \rho_{1\to0}$), (3) becomes
$\hat p = (1 - \rho_{01} - \rho_{10})p + \rho_{01}$ with **both slope and intercept** changed, so the
ranking is still preserved but **the meaning of the threshold changes entirely**. With
input-dependent noise ($\rho(g,o)$), even the ranking is not preserved — if the noise is larger on
hard grasps, the model systematically underestimates that region.

### R3 — the bound on the sim-to-real gap

**Covariate shift.** Under (A2) the difference between the real and simulated expected losses is

$$
\big|\mathbb{E}_{P_{real}}[\ell] - \mathbb{E}_{P_{sim}}[\ell]\big|
  = \left|\int \ell(o)\,\big(dP_{real} - dP_{sim}\big)\right|
  \le \lVert \ell\rVert_\infty \int \big|dP_{real} - dP_{sim}\big|
  = 2\lVert\ell\rVert_\infty\,\mathrm{TV},
$$

which with $\ell \in [0,1]$ is (5) (the constant is 1 or 2 depending on the definition of TV).

**Why it is vacuous.** A point cloud has thousands of dimensions, and if the two distributions have
**nearly disjoint supports** then $\mathrm{TV} \to 1$. The bound then says "the gap is at most 1",
which carries no information. Correcting by importance weights,
$\mathbb{E}_{P_{sim}}[\frac{dP_{real}}{dP_{sim}}\ell]$, is unbiased but its variance grows with
$\chi^2(P_{real}\Vert P_{sim})$, which also diverges in high dimension. **So one gives up on
tightening the bound and instead widens the support of $P_{sim}$** — that is the theoretical
motivation for domain randomization
([Tobin et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06907)).

**Where the assumption breaks**: if (A2) fails (the simulator's contact physics is wrong, so
$P_{sim}(y \mid g, o) \ne P_{real}(y \mid g, o)$), no reweighting helps. Reweighting can only fix a
difference in the **observation** distribution, never in the **conditional**. Contact-model error has
the same root as the pressure-distribution problem of `s2-contact-modes-dexterity`, so the
fundamental limit of sim-to-real lies not in the data but in the **model**.

## Proof sketch

### R2 — a unified proof of the two laws

**Hypotheses** H1: (A1) — the flip is a constant $\rho < 1/2$, independent of input and class and
independent across samples. H2: the score function $s(g, o)$ is given **independently** of the label
noise (the model was trained on clean labels, or only the evaluation labels are noisy). H3: positive
and negative pairs are exchangeable (no ties).
**Conclusion** C: (3) and (4a) hold, and with a balanced base rate both carry the **same shrinkage
factor** $(1-2\rho)$.

**Strategy**: derive both laws from **one line** — that the flip moves the expectation of the label
by an affine map. (3) applies that map to a conditional probability and (4a) to the indicator of a
pair.

1. **The affine map.** $\tilde y = y \oplus b$ with $b \sim \mathrm{Bern}(\rho)$ independent, so
   $\mathbb{E}[\tilde y \mid \cdot] = (1-2\rho)\mathbb{E}[y \mid \cdot] + \rho$. Conditioning on
   $(g,o)$ gives (3).
2. **Write AUC as an expectation over pairs.** $\mathrm{AUC} = \mathbb{P}[s_i > s_j \mid y_i = 1,
   y_j = 0]$, which by H2 and H3 can be written as the expectation of an indicator. With noisy
   evaluation labels, the class-conditional densities become mixtures with the marginal, and that
   mixing is the same affine map as in step 1.
3. **Collect the coefficients.** Expanding $A(u,v)$ bilinearly over the mixtures and using
   $A(f,f) = \frac12$ and $A(f_1,f) = \frac{\pi}{2} + (1-\pi)A$ gives (4a). At $\pi = \frac12$ the
   factor collapses to $1-2\rho$. (Omitted: handling ties and the finite-sample correction — dropping
   H3 requires the half-weight tie rule.)
4. **The common structure of the two laws.** That the shrinkage factors of (3) and (4) are the same
   $(1-2\rho)$ is no coincidence — both are the affine map of step 1 applied to a linear functional.
   So the conclusion is that **calibration and the ranking metric degrade at the same rate**.

**Removing a hypothesis**: dropping H2 so that **the training labels are noisy too**, the model has
already learned $\hat p$, so the shrinkage of (3) is inside its output and the factor in (4) becomes
$(1-2\rho)^2$. Dropping the symmetry of H1 separates slope from intercept and changes the meaning of
the threshold (§R2 failure modes). Dropping H3 lets ties pull the AUC further towards $\frac12$.

## Problem set

Difficulty: ★ 15 min / ★★ 1 hour / ★★★ a day or more.

**P1 (derivation, ★★, R2)** Derive (3) and find its general form for asymmetric flips
($\rho_{01} \ne \rho_{10}$). Write how **the meaning of the threshold 0.5** changes in that case.
*Solution sketch*: $\hat p = (1 - \rho_{01} - \rho_{10})p + \rho_{01}$. Since $\hat p = 0.5$
corresponds to $p = (0.5 - \rho_{01})/(1 - \rho_{01} - \rho_{10})$, setting the threshold at 0.5 is
**not** choosing a true probability of 0.5.

**P2 (derivation, ★★, R2)** Derive the $K$ of (4a) and confirm it collapses to $K = 1-2\rho$ at
$\pi = \frac12$. Then decide whether $K$ is smaller or larger than $1-2\rho$ for $\pi \ne \frac12$,
and show that with **both training and evaluation labels** noisy the factor becomes $(1-2\rho)^2$.
*Solution sketch*: expand the mixture densities by the bilinearity of $A(u,v)$ and use
$A(f,f) = \frac12$ and $A(f_1,f) = \frac\pi2 + (1-\pi)A$. For an unbalanced base rate $K$ depends on
$\pi$, so one **must not assume** the factor is $(1-2\rho)$ — that is the point of this item.
Numerical check (positive rate $\pi = 0.2654$, $\mathrm{AUC}_{true} = 0.9008$): the measurements
$0.8428/0.7910/0.7033/0.5626$ match the $K$ prediction of (4a),
$0.8423/0.7910/0.7037/0.5631$, to within $5\times10^{-4}$, and are **clearly different** from the
naive $(1-2\rho)$ prediction $0.8607/0.8206/0.7405/0.5802$ (here $K < 1-2\rho$).
With both labels noisy, the model output has already shrunk once so the factors multiply.

**P3 (numerical, ★, R2)** With $\mathrm{AUC}_{true} = 0.9068$, predict the measured AUC from (4) at
$\rho = 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4$ and compare with the reproduction task's measurements. What $\rho$ makes
the measured AUC look like 0.75?
*Answer*: predictions $0.8661, 0.8254, 0.7441, 0.5814$; measurements
$0.8662, 0.8252, 0.7432, 0.5810$ (largest difference $9\times10^{-4}$). From
$0.75 = 0.5 + (1-2\rho)(0.4068)$, $1-2\rho = 0.6146$ and $\rho = 0.1927$. Tolerance $10^{-3}$.
**Interpretation**: 19% label noise alone makes an "AUC 0.91 model" look like an "AUC 0.75 model".

**P4 (numerical, ★★, R1)** Using the table of the reproduction task, write the **confusion matrix**
of labelling by the sign of the nominal $Q$, taking $\Pi > 0.5$ as ground truth. Find the
false-negative rate and say how it affects the dataset's positive ratio.
*Answer*: the configurations with $\Pi > 0.5$ are $\Delta\theta = 3.142, 2.642, 2.600, 2.242$ (four),
and the rest are $2.200\,(\Pi=0.47)$ and $1.942\,(\Pi=0.195)$ (two). Labelling by $Q > 0$ makes the
first four positive and the last two negative, so in this table there are **no false negatives**.
But taking the $\Pi$ threshold at $0.4$ makes $\Delta\theta = 2.200$ ($\Pi = 0.47$) true while
$Q < 0$, giving a **false-negative rate of 1/5 = 20%**. In other words **the choice of threshold
changes the labels** — tolerance: exact counts.

**P5 (proof, ★★, R3)** Derive (5) and show that the bound is trivial when $\mathrm{TV} = 1$. Then
show that the variance of the importance-weighted estimator is proportional to the $\chi^2$
divergence.
*Strategy*: use the definition of total variation and $\lVert\ell\rVert_\infty \le 1$. Bound the
variance by $\mathrm{Var}[w\ell] \le \mathbb{E}[w^2] = 1 + \chi^2(P_{real}\Vert P_{sim})$.

**P6 (proof, ★★★, R2)** Handle the case where H2 is dropped: with the model trained on noisy labels
and evaluated on noisy labels, write both the optimal model output and the measured AUC in terms of
$\rho$. Then evaluate the claim that **noise in the training labels does not hurt the ranking**.
*Strategy*: the Bayes-optimal output of training is the $\hat p$ of (3), a monotone function of $p$,
so the ranking is preserved — **in the infinite-data limit only**. Note that with finite data the
noise inflates the estimation variance and the ranking does move.

**P7 (implementation, ★★, R1·R2)** Write (a) the label-noise experiment and (b) the robust-metric
calculator of the reproduction task. Requirements: compute AUC from a rank sum (stating the tie
rule), ECE over deciles, and reuse the LP tester of `s2-grasp-mechanics` for the robust metric.
*Rubric*: (a) the numbers of criteria 1–3 agree within tolerance; (b) you show ECE at $\rho = 0$ is of
order $0.003$ (finite-sample noise); (c) you show the standard error of $\Pi$ falls as $1/\sqrt{M}$
as the sample count goes $100 \to 1600$; (d) you print every configuration where the sign verdict of
$Q$ disagrees with the threshold verdict of $\Pi$.

**P8 (counterexample, ★★★, R1)** With three or more contacts, find a **rank inversion** (the nominal
$Q$ says better while the robust $\Pi$ says worse). Hint: a configuration with one nearly redundant
contact has a large $Q$ but is fragile under perturbation. Report the inversion as a function of
$\sigma$ (at what perturbation size does it appear?).
*Property checklist*: the zero inversions on the two-contact family is only a **negative result about
that family** (criterion 4 of the reproduction task). With three contacts, $Q$ is the distance to the
nearest facet, so a configuration with large margin in only one direction is possible, and if the
perturbation does not touch that direction $\Pi$ comes out low. If you find no inversion, **report
that you found none** and state the search range.

## Reproduction task

**The mandatory core is tier R2 (re-implementation); the R3 tier specified by the blueprint (applying
a pretrained model to a public point cloud) requires network access and model weights and is
therefore an optional task.** The mandatory part runs on CPU in under 5 minutes with numpy plus
scipy. The conditions of the optional part are in "optional R3" below.

**Input data (given by the lesson).**

*(a) Label noise.* Draw $2\times10^5$ latent qualities $q \sim U(0,1)$ and set the true success
probability $p = \mathrm{sigmoid}(8q - 4)$. Draw labels $y \sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$ and flip only the
evaluation labels with probability $\rho$. The score is $p$ itself (a perfect model). Seed 0.

*(b) Robust metric.* The unit-disc, two-contact setting of `s2-grasp-mechanics` with $\mu = 0.5$.
Perturb the contact angles by $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25^2)$, decide force closure by the LP and average
over $M = 400$ draws. The configurations are $\theta_1 = 0$ fixed with
$\theta_2 \in \{\pi, 2.642, 2.600, 2.242, 2.200, 1.942\}$, seed $1000 + i$.

1. **The AUC shrinkage law (checking (4) of R2)** — $\mathrm{AUC}_{true} = 0.9068 \pm 0.002$, and at

3. **Robust versus nominal metric (checking R1)** — the $(Q, \Pi)$ of the six configurations agree

4. **Verdict disagreement (the heart of R1)** — confirm that the two configurations with $Q < 0$

## Open problems

- **(open problem) Standardizing the label-generation procedure** — as (3) and (4) show, neither the
  success rate nor the AUC can be compared when the label noise differs, yet $\rho$ for synthetic
  labels is set by the perturbation distribution and the threshold, which differ between papers.
  Combined with the four conditions of R4, **most numbers in current grasping benchmarks are
  incomparable**. Current approach: public benchmarks with a fixed evaluation procedure
  ([Fang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR42600.2020.01146)). What is missing: a procedure
  that **estimates and reports** the $\rho$ of label generation and corrects the metrics with it.
- **(open problem) Sim-to-real for the conditional distribution** — the failure mode of R3 is the
  crux. Reweighting fixes only the observation distribution and not the error in the contact physics,
  which has the same root as the pressure-distribution problem of `s2-contact-modes-dexterity` and
  does not disappear with more data. Current approaches: domain randomization
  ([Tobin et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06907)), learning directly from real data
  ([Levine et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02199),
  [Kalashnikov et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.10293)). What is missing: a way to **bound**
  the gap with the contact-model error modelled explicitly.
- **(open problem) Calibrating abstention** — grasp **selection** needs only the ranking, but
  **rejection** needs the absolute probability, which (3) destroys. Answering "is this grasp worth
  attempting?" needs a calibration corrected for label noise, and that cannot be done without knowing
  $\rho$. Current approach: recalibration from a small amount of real data. What is missing: a way to
  guarantee a rejection threshold without knowing $\rho$.
